This dissertation examines the concept that everyday habits have come to reflect political
identities. Research like this is important because it can provide insight into how polarisation
and political fervour might manifest in social and/or non-political domains, providing
scholars the opportunity to study these subjects through alternative dimensions, which may
yield valuable and novel insights. This paper identifies alcoholic beverage preference as a
potentially fruitful medium for this kind of analysis. It utilises cross-sectional survey data
provided by More in Common to test whether voting intention is associated with alcoholic
beverage preference. The findings suggest that while there is insufficient evidence to indicate
that alcoholic beverage preferences correlate with voting intention universally, there is
evidence to suggest that a preference for beverages that are culturally associated with status
or tradition, such as Pimm’s and gin and tonic, are positively associated with right voting
intention, and negatively associated with left voting intention. It is also noted that this
relationship is comparable in magnitude to that of more traditional demographic variables
such as sex and education, indicating that some alcoholic beverages may possess meaningful
predictive value as lifestyle-based signals of political orientation. These results, though
modest, support the utility of researching lifestyle characteristics as markers of political
identity and polarisation, inviting further research.
